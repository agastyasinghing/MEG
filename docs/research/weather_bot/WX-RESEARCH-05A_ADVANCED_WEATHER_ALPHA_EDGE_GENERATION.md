# WX-RESEARCH-05A: Advanced Weather Alpha / Edge Generation

## Context and guardrails

### Executive summary

Building on the prior handoff, the strongest **plausible** weather-market alpha categories are not “better generic forecasts” in the abstract. They are source-conditioned hypotheses: whether the market is pricing the broad public weather story while the contract actually resolves on a narrower object such as a specific station, a specific National Weather Service climate product, full source precision, a freeze-time snapshot, or a post-analysis classification product. Current Kalshi weather help materials say weather contracts generally settle on the NWS final climate report issued the following morning, while current certified contract terms show that some weather contracts resolve on the full precision reported by the source agency and may ignore quality-control adjustments made after the expiration time. NOAA’s NOWData FAQ also states that NOWData includes archived **and preliminary** holdings, whereas NCEI Climate Data Online is the official quality-controlled archive. That combination makes resolution-source compatibility the most important early research lane. citeturn13search5turn16search7turn9search1turn9search6

The most dangerous false-edge categories are the ones where the analysis layer is built on the wrong data object: interpolated point data instead of the station named by the resolver, a gridded model history instead of the official observed archive, a city-level forecast instead of an airport climate station, a preliminary operational page instead of the final archive, or a rounded media display instead of source-agency full precision. Current provider documentation makes those mismatches concrete: Meteostat point data are interpolated from multiple stations and adjusted for distance and altitude; its default quality settings can combine observations with model-derived data; Open-Meteo automatically stitches multiple model runs and selects the highest-resolution model for each point; and WeatherAPI explicitly states that its “historical weather” product is archived forecast data, not actual observations. These are not implementation details. They are settlement-risk objects. citeturn17search0turn17search4turn17search1turn17search6turn17search2

The most credible timing-related hypotheses are also source-defined. Official schedules exist for major forecast and observation layers: GFS and GEFS run at 00/06/12/18 UTC, ECMWF medium-range forecasts are initialized at 00 and 12 UTC with supplementary 06 and 18 UTC runs, HRRR updates hourly and assimilates radar every 15 minutes, LAMP updates hourly for most elements, NDFD XML updates at roughly :25 and :55 past the hour, ASOS collects observations continuously and issues hourly and special observations, NEXRAD products update every volume scan, NHC full advisories are normally every six hours with intermediate advisories every three hours when watches or warnings are in effect, and HURDAT2 is produced from post-storm analysis rather than real-time advisories. These release clocks create plausible information windows, but only if the venue’s close time, determination logic, and source family are aligned with the signal being watched. citeturn1search0turn1search8turn1search2turn1search1turn18search1turn6search5turn20search0turn20search3turn1search3turn0search5turn8search18

The alpha categories that most clearly require market-microstructure awareness are stale-price hypotheses, nowcasting hypotheses, and cross-market coherence hypotheses. Prediction-market and exchange literature consistently ties efficiency to liquidity, spreads, and depth; Kalshi’s own help and fee materials show that immediate matches incur trading fees, quick orders can sweep several price levels, the displayed close time can differ from determination time, and market makers earn the spread. Recent Kalshi research further finds maker-side trades outperform taker-side trades on average and documents a favorite-longshot bias in contract prices. That means many “theoretical” weather edges will be non-executable after spreads, fees, fill risk, and adverse selection are included. citeturn12search0turn13search3turn13search12turn13search2turn13search4turn14search3turn14search6

The categories that should be deferred are the ones that depend on sparse, late, or classifier-driven labels: severe-event occurrence markets tied to preliminary versus final severe databases, tail-event and catastrophe-adjacent markets, cross-venue comparison without exact rule normalization, and behavioral or narrative mispricing claims that lack weather-specific validation. The standalone Weather Bot PRD should therefore emphasize a staged maturity path: first build trap-aware source filters and source-compatible labeling; then study threshold and timing effects; then test microstructure-adjusted hypotheses on historical market data; and keep all output human-review-only until WX-RESEARCH-06 validates event mapping, timestamp integrity, calibration, and execution realism. No implementation, forecast pulling, connector approval, trading, order placement, or autonomy is approved here. citeturn7search3turn7search1turn8search18turn15search2turn15search5turn14search1

### Source-compatibility edge

Weather-derivatives literature is useful here because it formalizes an analogue of the same problem this packet is trying to avoid: **basis risk**. CME weather products settle on standardized weather indexes tied to named cities/stations, and derivative literature describes “location basis risk” as the mismatch between the reference station/index and the actual site or exposure of interest. That literature does not prove profitable weather event trading, but it does confirm the structural mechanism: when the settlement object is standardized and geographically narrower than the trader’s mental model, mismatch risk is real. For Weather Bot, that means source compatibility is not just a back-office audit feature. It is the first screen that determines whether any alpha hypothesis is even intelligible. citeturn11search0turn11search5turn11search4turn11search1

The practical consequence is that *matching the actual resolution source can matter more than generic forecast accuracy*. For daily station temperature markets, the relevant comparison may be the station’s official climate report or published source-agency snapshot, not a city-level “current conditions” page. For daily or monthly precipitation and snowfall, the relevant comparison may be the product-specific treatment of trace precipitation, missing days, or first-posted versus quality-controlled values. For tropical-cyclone markets, the operative source can flip from advisory-time status to post-storm best-track status. For severe-occurrence questions, SPC preliminary reports and the NCEI Storm Events archive are different products with different timing and verification semantics. All of those are source-compatibility questions before they are forecasting questions. citeturn13search5turn16search7turn16search9turn8search1turn8search18turn7search3turn7search1

A useful PRD rule follows from that: if the analysis source does not preserve source identity, station identity, window identity, and revision identity, its output should be downgraded to a **source-risk warning** rather than promoted to an alpha note. Current provider docs justify that rule. Meteostat can interpolate across nearby stations and mix in model-derived data unless observation-only settings are enforced. Open-Meteo’s historical products can be reanalysis-based, and its live forecast endpoint automatically selects and stitches models. WeatherAPI’s history product is forecast archive, not actuals. Those are valuable research inputs in some contexts, but they are not automatically settlement-equivalent. citeturn17search0turn17search4turn17search1turn17search6turn17search2

### Threshold and near-boundary edge

Threshold markets are discontinuous settlement objects. Certified Kalshi weather contract language states that resolution is based on the **full precision** reported by the source agency and that rounding by media outlets or third-party summaries does not affect resolution. The same filing family also states that some post-expiration quality-control adjustments are not considered and that the data “as it appeared” at the expiration time governs. Another certified weather filing shows that missing monthly days can be treated as 0.0 inches in a NOWData sum calculation until fallback logic is applied. Near a strike, those semantics can move payout probability far more than a small change in a generic forecast mean. That is exactly the operational overlap with WX-RESEARCH-04’s handoff: the relevant object is the probability that the **venue-defined source/station/window/threshold rule** resolves Yes, not the probability that an abstract meteorological variable crosses a generic threshold. citeturn16search7turn16search9turn9search1

Forecast-verification literature reinforces the same lesson from another angle. Proper scoring rules, reliability diagrams, and EMOS-style postprocessing all exist because raw probabilistic forecasts are not automatically calibrated, particularly around thresholds and under changing spread conditions. In weather-market terms, the near-boundary problem is worse than ordinary calibration because the forecast distribution is layered on top of measurement and source semantics. A one-tenth-degree station difference, a trace-precipitation convention, or a wrong day window can move a threshold market from near-even to near-certain in settlement terms while barely changing the meteorological narrative. citeturn3search4turn3search1turn3search2turn2search0

The strongest near-boundary research candidates are therefore the markets that combine tight thresholds with source sensitivity: measurable-precipitation, 0.01-like rain thresholds, low-end snowfall, temperature buckets adjacent to round numbers, wind/gust cutoffs, and tropical/severe classification cutoffs keyed to advisory or archive status. The strongest near-boundary **no-trade** candidates are the same families whenever trace, full precision, source timing, or station identity are not fully pinned. citeturn16search7turn16search9turn8search18turn7search3

## Microstructure, validation, and PRD implications

### Market microstructure and execution-risk concepts

Weather-market edge cannot be evaluated credibly without execution realism. The prediction-market literature uses quoted spread, market depth, and trading volume as core liquidity measures, and Kalshi’s own market design reinforces why that matters: immediate matches incur trading fees, quick orders can execute across multiple price levels, and the displayed close time may differ from the eventual determination time. Recent Kalshi research also finds maker-side trades outperform taker-side trades on average and documents favorite-longshot bias in contract prices. Together, those sources imply a strong design rule for Weather Bot: any future alpha note should be treated as *gross informational discrepancy* until it is haircut by spread, fees, fill probability, and adverse-selection risk. citeturn12search0turn13search3turn13search12turn13search2turn14search3turn14search6

A second implication is that weather markets may be most fragile exactly when the signal is best. Fresh model runs, new radar updates, or first-posted climate reports can arrive when books are thin, when price depth is one-sided, or when waiting with a passive order risks non-execution before the venue closes. This is why “stale price alpha” and “liquidity/spread-aware adjustment” should be treated together rather than separately. The stronger the information shock, the more likely the book is to thin, widen, or disappear. citeturn12search0turn13search9turn13search14turn14search6

### Cross-market and correlated-event edge

Cross-market weather edge should be framed as **event-graph consistency research**, not as blanket arbitrage language. Prediction-market research shows that related contracts can become incoherent and that cross-platform price disparities can persist even in modern markets. Weather analogues include threshold ladders at the same station, closely related range buckets, neighboring airport markets under the same synoptic setup, and storm-state graphs such as “forms,” “reaches hurricane strength,” and “makes landfall.” But weather literature and weather-derivatives basis-risk work both warn that correlation is not equivalence: standardized reference stations can diverge from nearby locations, and narrow-band lake-effect or sea-breeze setups can make adjacent locations behave very differently. citeturn15search5turn15search9turn15search2turn11search4turn11search1turn5search4turn21search0

For Weather Bot, that means related-market hypotheses should only survive into later research if the following are all mapped explicitly: same or different source agency, same or different station, same or different observation window, same or different threshold semantics, same or different close/determination schedule, and same or different fallback chain. Until then, linked markets are best treated as *coherence checks* and *warning signals* rather than executable alpha candidates. citeturn16search13turn13search2turn15search0

### Human-review implications

Future human-review outputs should explain advanced alpha in a structured, fail-closed way. At minimum, each review note should identify: the alpha hypothesis type; the exact source compatibility status; the active official timing layer; the market’s station/window/threshold mapping; the active false-edge risks; the validation state; the confidence status; and a clear `no-trade`, `caution`, or `blocking` note when appropriate. The review should also say explicitly whether the signal is coming from an official settlement-adjacent source, a non-equivalent convenience source, or a purely methodological analogy. That structure follows directly from the rule-summary emphasis in venue docs and from the documented differences among official archives, operational products, and commercial convenience layers. citeturn16search13turn13search2turn9search1turn17search4turn17search2

A useful PRD sentence would be: *“Weather alpha notes are explanations of source-conditioned hypotheses, not approvals for action; any hypothesis lacking explicit source compatibility, threshold semantics, or execution feasibility must be downgraded to a caution or blocking note.”* That is a design recommendation derived from this research packet, not an implementation approval.

### Validation requirements before implementation

Before any advanced alpha concept becomes implementation-adjacent, WX-RESEARCH-06 should validate six things simultaneously.

First, it needs **source-compatible historical data**. That means the exact settlement object, not a convenience proxy: official archive layer, station identity, day window, threshold semantics, trace and missing-data handling, revision/freeze rule, and classification authority where applicable. NOAA and venue documentation show that these layers differ materially. citeturn9search1turn9search6turn8search18turn7search1turn16search7turn16search9

Second, it needs **historical market data with timestamps and depth**. Without quote history, close-time fields, and ideally order-book depth, no timing, stale-price, or microstructure hypothesis can be tested honestly. Prediction-market efficiency and Kalshi-specific research both make that point indirectly by tying performance to liquidity, maker/taker structure, and quote behavior. citeturn12search0turn14search3turn14search6

Third, it needs **clean event mapping and trap-filtered labels**. Weather Bot should not evaluate generic weather outcomes against market outcomes; it should evaluate market outcomes against source-compatible labels built from the exact resolver chain. This is the only defensible way to avoid lookahead leakage from later data corrections or from using the wrong official layer. citeturn16search7turn9search1turn10search2turn10search6

Fourth, it needs **calibration-first testing**. Proper scoring rules, reliability checks, threshold-bucket analysis, and out-of-sample validation are necessary even before trading friction is added. Forecast literature is clear that raw ensemble or model output is not automatically calibrated. citeturn3search4turn3search1turn3search2turn2search0

Fifth, it needs **transaction-cost realism**. Gross informational discrepancies must be translated into net executable discrepancies after fees, spread, slippage, fill risk, and adverse selection. Otherwise the system will overstate opportunities in precisely the low-liquidity settings where weather markets may be most prone to stale pricing. citeturn13search3turn13search12turn12search0turn14search6

Sixth, it needs **human-review safety**. Even a well-calibrated and source-compatible hypothesis should remain advisory until a reviewer can understand why it exists, what would invalidate it, and whether the venue’s rule text still matches the assumed source chain. That is a PRD-level governance requirement, not a model metric. citeturn16search13turn16search5turn13search2
