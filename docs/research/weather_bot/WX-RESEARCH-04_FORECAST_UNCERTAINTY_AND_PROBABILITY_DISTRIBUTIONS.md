# WX-RESEARCH-04: Forecast Uncertainty + Probability Distribution Research

## Executive framing and guardrails

### Executive summary

The main finding is that MEG should not treat a weather market probability as a single meteorological number. For threshold markets, the probability of a “Yes” outcome is a layered object: a forecast distribution for the physical variable, plus a resolution model for the exact source, station or grid-point mapping, time window, threshold semantics, trace and missing-data handling, revision policy, event classification rule, and venue resolution behavior. WX-RESEARCH-03 is the key handoff here: it shows that source identity, station identity, day-definition, trace treatment, archive finality, and event-classification authority can each change the resolved answer, not just the estimated weather state. In other words, a credible market probability must separate meteorological uncertainty from resolution uncertainty rather than blending them into one opaque percentage. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file1 fileciteturn0file2

For early PRD language, the strongest candidate methods are: source-compatible climatology baselines; short-horizon persistence baselines where the variable is persistent; simple calibrated threshold models such as logistic/probit or isotonic-style calibration on top of source-compatible features; and, later, ensemble-based probabilities with statistical post-processing such as EMOS/NGR or related calibrated distributional models. These methods are comparatively well studied, interpretable, and consistent with how operational probabilistic weather forecasting is evaluated. citeturn16academia4turn5academia0turn40academia2turn24academia9turn35academia2

Methods that look promising but should be deferred for early implementation are: full extreme-value pipelines for sparse tail markets, hierarchical source-uncertainty models, conformal wrappers for operational market probabilities, and flexible neural or flow-based distributional post-processing. The research literature shows these can improve calibration or tail behavior in some settings, but they require stronger validation discipline, larger training archives, or assumptions that are fragile when the market target itself is uncertain because of source or resolution semantics. citeturn21academia1turn7academia3turn26academia2turn33academia0

MEG should eventually verify probabilistic forecasts with proper scoring rules and calibration diagnostics. For binary threshold markets, Brier score and log score are the most natural headline metrics; for full continuous predictive distributions, CRPS is the standard baseline; for calibration, reliability diagrams, calibration curves, PIT or rank-histogram style diagnostics, and threshold-bucket backtests are essential. For extreme or near-threshold use cases, threshold-weighted scoring should be considered, but with the explicit warning that weighting the tail can create body-tail tradeoffs. citeturn32search8turn30search0turn3academia0turn23academia0turn23academia2turn23academia3turn21academia1turn35academia1

The threshold-market-specific concern is not merely “how uncertain is the forecast?” but “how much probability mass sits close enough to the threshold that source, station, time-window, rounding, trace, or finality choices can flip the settled answer?” This is most acute for measurable-precipitation markets, snowfall markets, wind/gust thresholds, and event-classification markets such as tornado or hurricane-status questions. WX-RESEARCH-03 and WX-RESEARCH-02 materially strengthen that conclusion. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file1

The standalone Weather Bot PRD should therefore say, before any implementation work, that MEG’s probability layer must be trap-aware, source-aware, and calibration-first; that raw forecast output is insufficient; that “edge” must not be claimed before source-compatible historical replay and threshold-bucket validation; and that human-review outputs should expose the active uncertainty modes rather than collapsing them into a single number. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file2

### Probability problem framing for weather markets

A weather threshold market is a binary event of the form “Does the resolved value of variable \(Y\) satisfy comparator \(c\) against threshold \(t\) over window \(w\), at location or station \(s\), under source chain \(r\)?” That differs from the simpler statistical question “What is the distribution of the atmospheric variable \(X\)?” because \(Y\) is often a source-specific, station-specific, window-specific, and sometimes revised or classified measurement of \(X\), not \(X\) itself. WX-RESEARCH-01 through WX-RESEARCH-03 consistently show that this mismatch is central for weather markets. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file1 fileciteturn0file2

Point forecasts are therefore insufficient for at least four reasons. First, threshold markets require the probability mass around a boundary, not just a mean or median. Second, the market target can differ from the forecast target because of station versus city, observation versus archive, advisory versus best-track, or preliminary versus final semantics. Third, errors grow with forecast horizon and raw ensembles are not automatically calibrated. Fourth, near-threshold markets can be dominated by non-meteorological uncertainty modes even when the physical forecast is reasonably sharp. citeturn35academia2turn24academia9turn5academia0turn40academia3 fileciteturn0file0

The operational framing MEG should use is:

\[
P(\text{market Yes}) = \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} P(r)\,P\!\left(g_r(Y_r)\!=\!\text{Yes}\mid r,\text{forecast data}\right),
\]

where \(r\) indexes plausible resolution scenarios such as source product, station mapping, day boundary, comparator interpretation, trace treatment, revision state, or classification authority. The point is conceptual, not implementation-ready: market probabilities should be built as **forecast distributions conditioned on explicit resolution scenarios**, not as raw weather probabilities with semantic uncertainty swept into the residual. That framing is especially aligned with the trap taxonomy in WX-RESEARCH-03 and with Bayesian recalibration ideas in probabilistic forecast research. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn15academia1turn33academia0

### Threshold probability modeling

For a continuous predictive distribution \(F\), the clean mathematical object is \(P(X \ge t)=1-F(t^-)\) or \(P(X>t)=1-F(t)\). In practice, weather markets often resolve on a *measured and rounded* value \(Y\), not latent \(X\), so the correct probability becomes a function of instrument precision, archive rounding, unit conversion, and comparator semantics. Near a threshold, those details can dominate. A model that estimates \(P(X>t)\) but the market resolves on \(Y \ge t\) after rounding can be directionally wrong even if the weather model is otherwise good. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn16academia4turn23academia0

MEG should therefore reason about threshold probability in five layers. First, specify the exact event as comparator plus threshold plus units. Second, choose or fit a predictive distribution for the weather quantity. Third, map the latent variable into the observation or archive process actually used by the source. Fourth, apply the resolution policy for trace, missing, or revised values. Fifth, only then compare to the market-implied probability. This staged reasoning is especially important when the probability mass is concentrated near the strike. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file1

Under station uncertainty, the threshold probability should be a mixture across plausible stations or mappings, not a single-site number. Under source uncertainty, it should be a mixture across plausible operative products or archive layers. Under time-window uncertainty, it should be a mixture across plausible boundary definitions if the market wording is not perfectly pinned. In all three cases, if the venue rule already fixes the relevant choice, the uncertainty should be collapsed; if it does not, MEG should expose the ambiguity rather than silently selecting one interpretation. fileciteturn0file0

For precipitation and snowfall, the probability mass near zero is unusually important. A yes/no “measurable precipitation” market can be extremely sensitive to trace convention and missing-value handling. For wind and gust, the upper tail and measurement interval matter. For temperature, threshold fragility is often a bias and station-mapping issue rather than a zero-inflation issue. For storm classification markets, the “threshold” may itself be an event label assigned by an authority rather than a raw physical threshold. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn5academia0turn40academia3turn33academia0

## Ensemble post-processing, calibration, scoring, and validation

### Ensemble forecasting and post-processing

Ensembles remain the standard meteorological machinery for representing flow-dependent forecast uncertainty, because they expose a range of plausible future states rather than a single trajectory. But the literature and benchmark work surfaced in this pass repeatedly show that raw ensemble output is often biased, under-dispersive, or otherwise miscalibrated, which is why post-processing is not optional if MEG ever reaches an ensemble-based stage. citeturn35academia2turn24academia9turn33academia1turn40academia3

For MEG, the operational interpretation should be:

- **ensemble spread** is evidence about forecast uncertainty, not proof of calibration;
- **underdispersion** means the ensemble is too narrow relative to realized outcomes;
- **overdispersion** means it is too wide;
- **bias correction** is needed when the ensemble mean or member distributions are systematically shifted;
- **post-processing** translates raw member output into a calibrated predictive distribution or calibrated event probabilities. citeturn24academia9turn5academia0turn40academia2turn40academia3

EMOS/NGR is the clearest classical route: fit a parametric predictive distribution whose location and scale depend on ensemble summaries, sometimes augmented spatially or topographically. Weather applications show this working for temperature, precipitation, wind speed, and even wind vectors, with local or spatial adaptation improving site-level performance. citeturn5academia0turn40academia2turn2academia5turn6academia0turn33academia3

Bayesian model averaging and related combination methods provide another route, particularly when the goal is to combine different models or ensemble members into a calibrated distribution. Multivariate extensions and copula approaches can restore dependency structure that is lost when each marginal variable is post-processed separately. Ensemble copula coupling is especially important as a conceptual warning: a system can have calibrated marginals but still misrepresent the multivariate dependence structure that matters for multi-threshold or compound markets. citeturn17academia3turn17academia0turn17academia1turn12academia1

For severe weather and other event-proxy problems, direct calibration of ensemble-derived hazard features into event probabilities can materially improve reliability. The surfaced WoFS work found that machine-learning calibration and logistic regression could generate more reliable severe-weather probabilities than a simple updraft-helicity-threshold baseline. That is a strong argument for not equating raw proxy exceedance frequencies with final market probabilities. citeturn15academia2turn24academia8

“Ensemble dressing” is relevant historically as a family of methods that turn discrete members into smooth uncertainty distributions, but this pass did not retrieve a strong primary source specifically on dressing. The safe takeaway is therefore limited: smoothing or probabilistic reinterpretation of members is a known part of post-processing practice, but MEG should not commit to a specific dressing method without a dedicated validation pass. **Confidence status: unclear.** citeturn12academia1turn24academia9

Forecast-horizon decay should be assumed, not discovered late. As lead time grows, calibration tends to worsen, and the marginal contribution of regime-specific information can shrink toward climatology. Seasonal and subseasonal work also shows that anomaly definitions and limited hindcast samples can distort reliability if handled inconsistently. citeturn25academia0turn15academia1turn10academia1

**Future researcher reading list.** Ensemble forecasting and post-processing: WeatherBench Probability; precipitation EMOS; Bayesian spatial temperature EMOS; wind-vector EMOS; ensemble copula coupling; severe-weather calibration papers. citeturn24academia9turn5academia0turn40academia2turn6academia0turn12academia1turn24academia8

### Backtesting and validation methodology preview

Historical replay should be designed around **what the market would have resolved to using the actual operative source chain**, not around whatever weather archive is easiest to fetch. WX-RESEARCH-02 and WX-RESEARCH-03 show why: provider/source incompatibility and revision/finality mismatch can make a backtest look better than a real market-facing system would perform. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file1

Train/test separation should be done by **date** and, where relevant, by **station or region**, because nearby stations and adjacent dates are not independent in the sense a market model needs. Local models may also overfit if station history is short. Cross-station borrowing can help, but only if the site heterogeneity is explicitly modeled. citeturn33academia3turn40academia2turn33academia0

At minimum, a future validation design should include: rolling-origin date splits; threshold-bucket evaluation; station-family splits where station identity matters; source-compatible ground truth; horizon-stratified calibration checks; and explicit tests for leakage from revised archives or future-corrected products. Seasonal or subseasonal systems should additionally validate anomaly definitions and hindcast sample sufficiency. citeturn10academia1turn15academia1turn24academia9

Leakage risks are substantial. Using final archives for a market that would have settled on preliminary daily reports, or using a station mapping that was not explicit at listing time, will overstate model quality. Overfitting risks are also material for logistic threshold models and rare-event classifiers, especially when event counts are small relative to feature counts. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn16academia4turn15academia2

Extreme-event scarcity is a structural limitation, not a temporary inconvenience. Tail events are rare, sample sizes are small, and regime shifts matter. As a result, any tail-heavy method such as EVT or threshold-weighted training should be tested with substantially more caution than body-of-distribution models. citeturn21academia1turn35academia1

## Trap-aware market implications and roadmap

### Trap-aware probability adjustment

A trap-aware probability pipeline should distinguish five layers:

| Layer | What belongs here | MEG implication |
|---|---|---|
| forecast uncertainty | Distribution of the future weather variable conditional on forecast inputs | Use ensembles, calibrated regressions, analogs, or other probabilistic methods. |
| measurement uncertainty | Instrument, observation protocol, trace handling, missing data, and local observing practice | Treat trace and missing values as explicit resolution rules, not residual noise. |
| source uncertainty | Which product, archive layer, or page is operative | Use source-scenario mixtures or fail closed when the source is ambiguous. |
| mapping uncertainty | Station or location mapping, day boundary, units, and comparator semantics | Carry these as event-definition choices before computing threshold probabilities. |
| market-resolution uncertainty | Venue fallback chain, discretion, invalidation, or source replacement | Keep as a separate final-layer uncertainty and expose it to human review. |

This layered framing is the safest way to use WX-RESEARCH-03 operationally. It prevents the common mistake of treating semantic traps as “extra weather noise.” They are not. They are separate latent variables that mediate how a physical forecast maps into a market payoff. fileciteturn0file0

Practically, that means probability adjustment should be scenario-based. If the market could plausibly resolve on two different stations, compute probabilities for both and either weight them explicitly or mark the market unresolved for automation. If a daily precipitation market is ambiguous about trace handling, produce separate conditional probabilities under each trace rule. If storm classification could be advisory-based or post-analysis-based, again separate the scenarios. Only forecast uncertainty belongs inside the weather-distribution model itself. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file1

This section also clarifies which methods help separate the uncertainty types:

- Standard forecast methods such as ensembles, EMOS, quantile models, and logistic threshold models mainly address **forecast uncertainty**.
- Hierarchical and Bayesian scenario models can partially separate **station**, **source**, **time-window**, **revision**, or **classification** uncertainty *if* those are explicit modeled states.
- No surfaced standard method solved **market-resolution uncertainty** in the venue-specific sense; that remains a MEG-specific research problem. citeturn15academia1turn33academia0 fileciteturn0file0

### Market edge and risk implications

A forecast probability can differ from a market-implied probability for two very different reasons. One is real forecast disagreement about the physical event. The other is semantic disagreement about what the market will actually settle on. WX-RESEARCH-02 and WX-RESEARCH-03 show that the second kind of disagreement can create **false edge**: the model may appear smarter than the market only because it is forecasting a cleaner or different target. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file1

Near-threshold markets are especially fragile. When the predictive density is concentrated around the strike, small changes in station selection, archive revision, trace policy, or rounding can produce large shifts in \(P(\text{Yes})\). In those regimes, even a “good” weather model does not justify confidence unless the market-target ontology is pinned down exactly. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn23academia0turn21academia1

Liquidity, spread, and fees also matter conceptually, because they make the economically relevant decision threshold differ from the naïve raw probability difference between a model and a quote. But this report does **not** recommend any trade or order behavior. The narrower research point is that a human-review output should show both the modeled meteorological probability and the active semantic-risk flags, so the reviewer can see whether an apparent discrepancy is weather-driven or resolution-driven. fileciteturn0file2 fileciteturn0file0

### Final handoff summary

The methods MEG should recommend for the standalone PRD are: source-compatible climatology and persistence baselines; simple threshold-event models with explicit calibration; and, after stronger source-compatible archives exist, ensemble-based probabilities with EMOS/NGR-style post-processing. Bayesian scenario layers and hierarchical models should stay on the roadmap as research candidates for later source-awareness and station-awareness, not as early implementation commitments. citeturn16academia4turn5academia0turn40academia2turn15academia1turn33academia0

The methods to defer are: EVT-heavy tail pipelines as a first implementation, conformal prediction as a market-probability wrapper, flexible neural or flow-based probabilistic models, and bespoke source-uncertainty adjustment engines. Each is intellectually relevant, but each carries a validation burden that is too high for an early Weather Bot PRD claiming operational readiness. citeturn21academia1turn7academia3turn26academia2

The key uncertainty risks are: source mismatch, station ambiguity, time-window mismatch, threshold and rounding ambiguity, trace or missing-value treatment, revision or finality mismatch, event-classification ambiguity, and venue-resolution behavior. These are not mere implementation details. They are part of the probability problem itself. fileciteturn0file0
