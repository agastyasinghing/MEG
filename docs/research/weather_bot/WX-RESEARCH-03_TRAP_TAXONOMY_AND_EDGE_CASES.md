# WX-RESEARCH-03: Weather Market Trap Taxonomy + Example Markets

## Executive summary and reconciliation

WX-RESEARCH-01 and WX-RESEARCH-02 point in the same direction: weather markets are only clean enough for canonical event mapping when the contract pins a specific observable, a specific place or station, a specific time window, and a specific resolution product. WX-RESEARCH-01 established that this is why station-anchored temperature, precipitation, snowfall, and some hurricane-status markets are the best early candidates, while city-headline markets, third-party weather-page markets, creator-resolved markets, and catastrophe-adjacent markets are much riskier. WX-RESEARCH-02 tightened that conclusion by showing that many convenient weather providers do not reproduce venue resolution semantics because they interpolate, blend, remap, or archive forecast/model output rather than the primary source the venue actually uses. The combined conclusion is stronger than either packet alone: **for MEG, source identity is part of event identity**. fileciteturn0file1 fileciteturn0file0 citeturn20view1turn33view0turn35view3turn34view7

The most dangerous unresolved traps are the ones that collapse different observational ontologies into a single headline. The highest-risk examples are: city labels that silently stand in for an airport or climate station; severe-weather occurrence markets that do not say whether they mean preliminary SPC/WFO reports, final NCEI Storm Data/Storm Events records, or SPC monthly tornado counts; hurricane markets that do not say whether they key off advisory issuance time or later best-track/post-analysis products; daily or monthly precipitation/snow markets that do not specify how traces, missing values, and later revisions are handled; and any market that resolves by one source chain while the trading or analysis layer watches another. Those traps do not merely create analyst inconvenience; they can change the resolved answer. citeturn5view0turn4view0turn36view0turn8view1turn21view1turn9view0turn13view0

The most common traps are milder but still material: boundary semantics such as “at least” versus “more than,” local day versus UTC or climatological day, snow depth versus snowfall, wind gust versus sustained wind, and provisional versus final data. These often can be normalized later if the venue has already pinned the station, product, and freeze rule; absent that pinning, they escalate to blocking. citeturn29view3turn13view0turn4view0turn20view1

The highest trap-density market families are daily city-binary rain/snow markets, severe-occurrence markets, monthly precipitation and snowfall markets, third-party display-page markets, and international markets whose wording does not identify a country-specific official archive and day-definition. The practical implication for WX-RESEARCH-04 is that uncertainty modeling cannot stop at weather physics. It also has to model **source uncertainty, station uncertainty, window uncertainty, revision uncertainty, and classification uncertainty**. fileciteturn0file1 fileciteturn0file0 citeturn29view3turn30view3turn36view0turn45view1

## Scope and research method

This packet is **research only**. It does **not** approve connector implementation, external API calls, runtime execution, forecast pulls, trading, order placement, autonomy, or any production use of weather providers. The analysis below is designed to harden the MEG weather canonical-event layer before implementation work, not to green-light implementation. The uploaded planning docs named in the brief were not directly accessible in this workspace, so PRD-facing implications below are inferred from the current brief plus WX-RESEARCH-01 and WX-RESEARCH-02, rather than quoted from the PRD text itself. fileciteturn0file1 fileciteturn0file0

The method in this packet is deliberately conservative. It treats a market as unsafe for automation whenever any one of the following is unpinned: (a) the observation point, (b) the day/window definition, (c) the exact source product, (d) the fallback chain, (e) the revision/freeze rule, or (f) the event-classification authority. That approach is consistent with NOAA/NWS/NCEI source behavior, because operational products, archives, gridded forecasts, interpolated point products, and post-analysis records all have materially different semantics. citeturn46view0turn21view1turn20view0turn36view0turn35view3turn34view7

**Final handoff summary**

The traps that should **block future automation** are the ones that leave event identity under-specified: unspecified official source; city labels without pinned station IDs; any provider-versus-resolution mismatch where the provider is gridded, blended, interpolated, or archived-forecast data; severe-occurrence markets without an explicit authority layer; tropical-classification markets without an advisory-versus-post-analysis rule; trace/missing-value ambiguity; first-report-versus-final-archive ambiguity; venue fallback chains or source replacement powers; and international markets without a country-specific official product and day definition. These are not edge cases. They are common enough to corrupt canonical event mapping if normalized away. fileciteturn0file1 fileciteturn0file0 citeturn5view0turn4view0turn36view0turn21view1turn29view3turn30view3turn34view0turn34view7turn35view3
