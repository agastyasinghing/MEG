# WX-RESEARCH-02: Weather Provider/Source Compatibility Deep Research

## Executive summary and scope

**Executive summary.** The strongest source candidates for MEG’s Weather Bot canonical event graph are not general-purpose commercial weather APIs. They are the official NOAA/NWS/NCEI stack for U.S. point-weather markets, plus NHC for tropical-cyclone markets and SPC for severe-weather forecast/watch market families. The core reason is source compatibility: NWS exposes official forecasts, alerts, and station-linked observations; NCEI exposes archived station datasets and authentic archived copies; NHC publishes advisory archives, Tropical Cyclone Reports, and HURDAT2 best-track data; and SPC publishes convective outlooks, watches, and storm-report products. Those source families map far more cleanly to prediction-market resolution rules than consumer “city weather” APIs that silently blend models, stations, radar, and interpolation. citeturn52view0turn52view1turn52view2turn52view3turn55view2turn53view0turn53view1turn53view2turn53view3

Among non-government providers, the best later-stage candidates are **Meteomatics**, **Meteostat**, and **Open-Meteo**, but only for carefully limited roles. Meteomatics is unusually strong on source transparency because it documents explicit data-source identifiers, including `mix-obs` for station observations and `mm-mos` for MOS based on station observations, and it documents historical availability and source families. Meteostat is strong for historical/station-oriented work because it is explicitly observation-focused, exposes station metadata and upstream provider metadata, and warns that point-data can mix observations and model-derived data unless that is controlled. Open-Meteo is unusually transparent on model lineage, update cadence, grid-cell selection, time-zone handling, unit handling, and archived forecast access, but it is fundamentally a model/grid product rather than an official post-event observation/archive authority. citeturn47view3turn48view0turn49view0turn49view3turn49view5turn34view0turn34view1turn35view0turn35view1turn35view2turn18view0turn51view0turn51view1turn51view2turn51view5

The riskiest providers for resolution use are **WeatherAPI.com**, **OpenWeather**, and several “single-call city weather” services that do not cleanly preserve official-source semantics. WeatherAPI.com is the clearest red flag: its own pricing FAQ says its “historical weather” is an archive of forecast data and “not actuals,” which is a direct mismatch for observed-event resolution. OpenWeather documents a proprietary model, mixed “measured or calculated” values, and coordinate-based retrieval, but not a transparent station-selection path suitable for audit-grade point resolution. Weatherbit is better than many peers because it discloses upstream source families and even a historical revision-version API, but it still does not expose enough station-selection and aggregation detail to make it a primary resolution authority. citeturn30view0turn38view0turn39view0turn39view1turn37view0turn31view3turn31view4turn32view0

The biggest unresolved questions are not about forecasts. They are about **which official archive should resolve which market family**. Hourly temperature/wind markets are much easier because official station archives are well described. Daily precipitation and snowfall markets are harder because station choice, observation day, local-time windowing, and archive revision behavior matter. Severe-weather occurrence markets are harder still because SPC storm-report products are operationally useful, but this pass did not fully establish the final “official post-event archive” that should settle hail/tornado/wind occurrence markets after preliminary reporting. Tropical-cyclone markets are comparatively cleaner because NHC’s advisory archive, Tropical Cyclone Reports, and HURDAT2 are clearly documented and post-analysis oriented. citeturn55view2turn53view0turn53view1turn53view2turn53view3turn44view0

**Research scope and non-approvals.** This packet is research only. It does **not** approve connector implementation, external API calls in production, credentials or secrets, forecast pulls, runtime behavior, autonomous execution, trading, order placement, or autonomy. It also does **not** approve any provider for implementation. It only narrows the source-compatibility field for a later approval gate.

This packet also proceeds **without** direct access to PRD-P1-WX-01 / WX-RESEARCH-01. Where the prompt asked about canonical weather-event fields from WX-01, this report inferred compatibility from the market families and field categories named in the brief. That makes this packet useful for shortlist formation, but not sufficient by itself for connector approval.

## Evaluation framework

**Provider/source evaluation framework.** For MEG, the decisive question is not “who has the nicest weather API,” but “which provider can preserve enough source identity, field semantics, and archival traceability that a human reviewer could defend a market resolution later.” The dimensions below are therefore ordered around resolution-risk, not developer convenience.

| Dimension | Why it matters for MEG | What to look for in docs | Connection to market resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resolution-source compatibility | Markets often resolve against a named source, not generic “weather.” | Explicit archive/source names, official references, post-event archive semantics. | If the provider cannot reproduce the venue’s named source, the canonical graph will drift. |
| Official-source alignment | Official sources reduce dispute risk. | Government operation, primary archive status, explicit “official archive” language. | Needed for U.S. weather markets that point to NOAA/NWS/NHC/SPC-style authorities. |
| Data-source transparency | Hidden blending breaks auditability. | Source/methodology pages, explicit station/model/radar labels, revision notes. | Human review needs to explain not just the value, but where it came from. |
| Station/observation granularity | Point markets need point semantics. | Station IDs, observation-station lookup, station metadata, station lists in responses. | “City weather” is not the same thing as “station weather.” |
| Historical observation availability | Most disputes happen after the event. | Archive availability, historical range, revision/version identifiers, official copy language. | Final settlement needs durable post-event data, not ephemeral live endpoints. |
| Forecast horizon coverage | Some market families resolve on forecast issuance, not observation. | Forecast length, archived forecast issuance history, model-run retrieval. | Necessary for markets keyed to forecast content or historical forecast verification. |
| Timestamp/timezone semantics | Day boundary mistakes can change the answer. | Local-time vs UTC rules, timezone fields, offset fields, aggregation-window docs. | Daily rain/snow/temperature markets fail if windows are ambiguous. |
| Unit support | Threshold markets need exact unit conversions. | Explicit `metric`/`imperial`/`standard` or unit-group docs. | Prevents accidental threshold drift. |
| Precipitation support | Rain markets are common and difficult. | Hourly/daily precip, radar-vs-gauge notes, accumulation-window semantics. | Must know whether the value is gauge, radar estimate, model, or blended. |
| Snowfall support | Snow resolution is often more fragile than rain. | Snowfall, snow depth, snow-water-equivalent, station/reporting caveats. | Snow markets often fail under mixed model-vs-observation semantics. |
| Wind/gust support | Airport/station markets often use wind/gust thresholds. | Wind speed, gust, observation cadence, station-level availability. | Needs exact point observation semantics. |
| Temperature support | Core market family. | 2 m temperature, min/max/mean, hourly/daily, observation or model label. | Threshold settlement depends on aggregation and source identity. |
| Storm/hurricane support | Tropical markets need storm-object semantics. | Advisories, best tracks, intensity, track, landfall, GIS/archives. | NHC-like storm identity is far more robust than generic weather APIs. |
| Extreme-weather support | Severe-weather markets depend on event-classification authority. | Outlooks, watches, reports, archive products, event metadata. | Needs a documented path from operational product to archived post-event evidence. |
| Archive/auditability | Human reviewers need replayability. | Historical API, bulk archives, revision/version docs, official-copy disclaimers. | Core to the canonical event graph and later review. |
| Pricing/free-tier posture | Important later, but only after source fit. | Public pricing docs, free tiers, enterprise-only posture. | A source can be fit-for-purpose and still unsuitable operationally. |
| Rate limits | Hard limits can affect later connector design. | Public per-day/per-second limits, fairness language, 429 behavior. | Matters at approval stage, not source-selection stage. |
| Terms/commercial-use posture | Legal posture affects later connector approval. | License names, attribution requirements, redistribution restrictions. | Can block downstream use even when data fit is good. |
| Documentation quality | Poor docs increase hidden-resolution risk. | Endpoint docs, source docs, examples, revision notices. | If the methodology is undocumented, disputes get harder to resolve. |
| Reliability/failure modes | MEG needs fail-closed behavior later. | Status pages, outage notices, archive-vs-live distinctions. | Impacts review and later no-network test strategy. |
| Human-review explainability | Reviewers must justify outputs. | Station lists, source fields, archive provenance, readable semantics. | Best predictor of whether a reviewer can defend the call. |
| Future connector suitability | Later implementation should follow evidence. | Stable docs, auth model, observability hooks, fixture-friendly semantics. | This packet narrows candidates; it does not approve them. |

In the underlying sources, these dimensions show up repeatedly. NWS explicitly distinguishes point lookups, grid forecasts, stations, and archival limits. NCEI explicitly distinguishes archive status, authenticity, liability, download mechanisms, and delay. NHC explicitly distinguishes advisories, reports, and best-track archives. Stronger private vendors document model/source identifiers, station-use behavior, or historical revision/version behavior; weaker ones advertise convenience without preserving resolution-relevant provenance. citeturn52view1turn52view2turn52view3turn55view2turn53view0turn53view1turn48view0turn35view2turn42view0

**Official-source compatibility.** For MEG, “official source” should mean the source family named by the market rules or, if rules are silent, the source family most defensible as the primary government/agency authority for that event type. In U.S. weather markets, that typically means NWS for live official forecasts/alerts/observations, NCEI for archived atmospheric records, NHC for tropical cyclone advisories and post-analysis best track, and SPC for severe-weather outlook/watch products. citeturn52view0turn52view1turn52view3turn55view2turn53view0turn53view1turn53view2turn53view3

Station, gridded, and city-level data are not interchangeable. NWS forecast products are grid-based, roughly 2.5 km per cell, and `/points` must be used to discover the current grid mapping and station/zonal context for a coordinate. NCEI ISD is station-archive data, with station networks and archival delay semantics. By contrast, Open-Meteo documents grid-cell selection and statistical downscaling, Visual Crossing documents automatic best-source selection and mixed `obs`/`remote`/`fcst`/`stats` timelines, and Meteostat documents that point data may combine observation and model-derived values by default. Those are useful products, but they are not the same category of evidence as a named official station archive. citeturn52view2turn52view3turn55view2turn51view4turn51view5turn42view0turn43view0turn43view1turn35view2

Archive delay and revision behavior matter. NWS alert endpoints only retain seven days in the API and explicitly direct users to NCEI for official CAP archives. NCEI ISD says online data are typically delayed 24 hours and emphasizes archive/authentic-copy semantics. NHC’s Tropical Cyclone Reports include post-analysis best track, while HURDAT2 is explicitly a curated best-track dataset updated after season completion. These are favorable properties for market settlement because they separate live operations from finalized archival recordkeeping. citeturn52view3turn55view2turn53view0turn53view1

The practical implication for provider selection is straightforward: use official sources wherever the market rule can plausibly point to one, and only use private providers as secondary tools for mapping, enrichment, forecast comparison, or operator convenience. If a private provider silently substitutes model or blended “city weather” for official station or archive weather, it is a poor fit for canonical resolution, even if its developer experience is excellent. citeturn30view0turn39view0turn42view0turn35view2

For temperature and wind thresholds, the cleanest path is a named official station archive or an official station-linked source chain, because point identity and timestamp semantics are both well preserved there. NWS and NCEI expose that path directly, and Meteomatics/Meteostat can support it secondarily when MEG constrains them to station-observation semantics rather than generic point interpolation. citeturn52view2turn52view3turn55view2turn49view0turn35view1turn35view2

For precipitation and snowfall, the resolution-risk rises sharply because accumulation windows, local day boundaries, gauge-vs-radar substitution, and sparse-station filling all become material. Visual Crossing explicitly supports `obs`, `remote`, and even radar-preferred options; Weatherbit explicitly says historical data are sourced from stations, radar, and re-analyses; Meteostat explicitly warns that point data can blend observations and model-derived values. Those capabilities are useful operationally, but they show exactly why private aggregation services are dangerous if the venue rule expects one official observation source. citeturn43view0turn43view4turn31view3turn35view2

For hurricane markets, NHC is clearly the best fit. Its archive structure is inherently storm-centric, and its Tropical Cyclone Reports and HURDAT2 products are post-analysis and historically organized. No general weather API in this research pass was more source-compatible for that family. citeturn53view0turn53view1

For severe-weather markets, SPC is highly relevant for *forecast-family* markets because it explicitly publishes convective outlooks and current watches. The unresolved part is *occurrence-family* markets: this pass did not definitively settle whether final hail/tornado/wind occurrence should resolve against SPC storm reports, NCEI Storm Data / Storm Events, local WFO storm reports, or market-specific text. That unresolved archive question should block connector approval for severe occurrence markets until settled. citeturn53view2turn53view3

**Resolution-source mismatch risks.** The most common failure mode will be **city-vs-station mismatch**. OpenWeather geocoding, for example, can resolve ZIP codes to centroid coordinates, and many city APIs then return a provider-selected representation of conditions at or near that coordinate. That is not the same thing as “official weather at station X,” and the difference can decide a threshold market. citeturn39view4turn39view0

A second failure mode is **provider-selected station mismatch**. Visual Crossing is better than many providers because it can return `stations` and a `source` field, but it still auto-selects sources and interpolates. Meteostat is similarly strong on transparency, but it explicitly says point data can mix observations with model-derived data unless the caller constrains that behavior. Those are manageable risks only if the connector later locks them down. citeturn43view0turn43view3turn35view2

A third failure mode is **forecast-vs-observed confusion**. WeatherAPI.com makes the problem explicit: its historical product is forecast archive, not actual observations. Open-Meteo and OpenWeather also blend forecast/model concepts into “current” or “historical forecast” flows. Those can be valuable for forecast markets, but they are unacceptable substitutes for observed-event settlement unless the venue explicitly resolves against forecast output. citeturn30view0turn18view0turn51view1turn39view0

A fourth failure mode is **aggregation-window and timezone mismatch**. NWS grids, Visual Crossing local-time daily queries, Open-Meteo UTC offsets and local time zones, and archived station data all document different time semantics. If the canonical graph does not preserve the source’s local-day rule, daily rain/snow/temperature markets will eventually settle incorrectly. citeturn52view2turn43view1turn51view5turn44view0

A fifth failure mode is **archive and revision mismatch**. NWS live APIs are not the same thing as final archives; NCEI archive products are delayed and archived; NHC best tracks update after seasonal analysis; Weatherbit even exposes a revision-version concept for historical data. MEG’s canonical graph therefore needs a field that distinguishes live operational evidence from later archival evidence. citeturn52view3turn55view2turn53view0turn31view0

## Provider deep dives and method approaches

**Provider deep dives.**

**NOAA / NWS / NCEI.** Official docs consulted in this pass included the NWS API Web Service, the NCEI ISD dataset overview, and the GHCNm landing page. NWS gives MEG live forecast/grid/station context; NCEI gives archived station records, authentic-copy semantics, documented delay, and download mechanisms. This is the default primary stack for U.S. source-compatible resolution work. Recommendation: **candidate**. Confidence status: **confirmed**. Suggested future reading: NWS API Web Service; NCEI Global Surface Hourly current landing page and full format docs; NCEI Climate Data Online API docs; Local Climatological Data / GHCN-Daily docs; ASOS / station observing-program docs. citeturn52view0turn52view1turn52view2turn52view3turn55view2turn15view0
